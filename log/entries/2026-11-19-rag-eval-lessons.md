*Notes from rebuilding Historical Atlas, a question-answering app over 5,082 British parliamentary speeches and 15 US diplomatic documents on five League of Nations disputes (1920-1926). Code and numbers: [github.com/JBarens/historical-atlas](https://github.com/JBarens/historical-atlas).*

The version of Historical Atlas I deployed in August looked like a RAG app. It had a vector database, a retrieval step and citations under every answer. It was not doing retrieval. The embedding library was left out of the production build, so every document got a zero vector, every distance was equal, and the "retrieved" passages came back in whatever order the rows were stored. The answers came from the model's training data and the citations were text the model wrote. Nothing told me, because nothing measured it.

So the rebuild started with the eval, and the eval turned out to be most of the work. Claude Code wrote most of the code; the questions, the labels and the judging took most of my time.

## A label is a passage, not an answer

The eval set has 106 items: answerable questions, questions the corpus cannot answer, ambiguous ones, follow-ups, calculations and cross-case questions. Each answerable item is labelled with a verbatim passage that I checked against the source. That makes retrieval measurable: the passage is in the top 5 or it is not.

The labels were also the first thing to break. Chunking by speech, a better embedding model, hybrid BM25 plus dense search and reranking took recall at 5 on the dev set from 0.20 to 0.44. When I went through the 25 remaining misses, 19 were eval errors, not retrieval errors. I tightened the labelling rules and rebuilt the set. With the first labels I would have kept tuning against noise.

The split had its own bug. I grouped near-duplicate questions into clusters so that paraphrases could not land in both dev and test. Embedding only the question text put all four calculation items, which share a question and differ only in their history, into one cluster, and so all of them into dev. The test set has no calculations.

## The judge needed grading too

An LLM grades each answer as correct, partially correct or incorrect. Before trusting it I compared it with hand grading on 10 answers. The judge agreed with the hand grades on 5. All five disagreements went the same way: it marked honest partial answers as incorrect and scored a formatting error as a content error. After I rewrote the rubric, the same configuration went from 0.69 to 0.79 correctness without any change to the system. The judge setup moved the score as much as the change I was testing, so I only compare variants under the same judge and rubric.

## Retrieval is the ceiling

On dev, every question whose labelled passage reached the top 5 got an answer that was at least partly correct: 19 of 19. When the passage was missed, the system said it had insufficient evidence in all 6 cases instead of making something up. On the test set, 6 of the 8 failed answerable items were retrieval misses.

## Opening the test set once

I froze the pipeline at one commit, wrote down my predictions and ran the held-out test once. I predicted 0.79 correctness and got 0.80, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.68 to 0.93 on 20 answerable items. Faithfulness came in at 0.98 (61 of 62 claims supported) against my 1.00, and p95 latency at 5.4 s against my 4 s. A question costs about a cent.

The interval matters more than the point estimate. With n=20, "0.80" on its own claims more than the data supports. A held-out test also protects against one specific thing: tuning on the questions you report. Most of my questions were generated by a model from the passages that answer them, which flatters retrieval, and a clean split does nothing about that. Two test items I had labelled as out of corpus turned out to be answerable.

## Grounding is not truth

For security I chose containment over detection: retrieved documents are wrapped as data, code owns the control flow, and the one tool validates its inputs. On a small attack set (16 attacks, drafted with AI), 14 passed. The useful failure was a planted document saying the Aaland Islands had been awarded to Norway. The system repeated it, with a citation. Grounding stops instructions hidden in documents. It does not stop false facts in them, because answering from the documents is the job. In a client setting, where the documents are emails and tickets, whether the sources are right is a separate question that has to be asked.

## What I would change

More test questions written by hand, a slice labelled by someone other than me, and a larger test set so the interval narrows. The [demo](https://huggingface.co/spaces/JBarens310/historical-atlas) is static and replays real pipeline outputs.
