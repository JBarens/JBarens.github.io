*Research and interview preparation sample. Claims checked on 12 November 2026; sources at the end.*


## Who he is (30 seconds)
- Former governance researcher at OpenAI. Resigned in April 2024 "after losing confidence that the company would behave responsibly in its attempt to build AGI" (his own thread). Refused the non-disparagement agreement at the risk of losing his vested equity; OpenAI then said it would not enforce those agreements.
- One of 13 current and former OpenAI and Google DeepMind staff behind the open letter "A Right to Warn about Advanced Artificial Intelligence" (June 2024).
- Leads the AI Futures Project. Lead author of AI 2027 (April 2025), a month-by-month scenario of AI progress.
- Track record: his 2021 essay "What 2026 looks like" is widely cited as unusually accurate, which is why people take his timelines seriously.

## What AI 2027 actually claims (and what it doesn't)
- Core milestone: a "superhuman coder", an AI that can do any coding task the best lab engineer can, much faster and cheaper. Original median: March 2027.
- From there, AI that accelerates AI research itself. Their median from superhuman coder to superintelligence: about one year, "with wide error margins".
- It is a scenario with two endings (a race and a slowdown), not a prediction that everything happens exactly so. Media often report it as a date for doom; the authors frame it as one plausible path.
- Update, 10 Nov 2026 ("Q3.5 Timelines Update"): based on new METR data the team moved its median for a superhuman AI researcher to 2027; co-author Eli Lifland moved from about 2030 to 2027. Their claim is that reality is running a few months ahead of the scenario.

## Interview questions
1. Your 2021 forecast for 2026 held up well. What did you get wrong, and what does that tell us about how to read AI 2027?
2. Your key milestone is a superhuman coder. Why is coding the hinge, rather than, say, science or robotics?
3. The November update leans on METR's measurements of how long a task AI can complete autonomously. What would that curve have to do in 2027 for you to say "we were wrong"?
   - Follow-up: how much of that measured progress could be benchmark-specific rather than real-world capability?
4. In the scenario the decisive moment is inside a lab, not in public. What would an engineer inside a lab actually see first?
5. Many people who deploy AI in companies see systems that are still brittle and need heavy human checking. How do you square that with an intelligence explosion within about a year?
6. You gave up equity to speak freely. What can you still not say, and does that limit what the public knows?
7. The "slowdown" ending requires coordination between labs and governments. Name one concrete step that is realistic in the next 12 months.
8. For a European audience: does it matter at all where this happens, if the decisive labs are in the US and China?
9. What would change your mind fastest: a technical result, a policy event, or something else?

## Claims that are often reported wrongly
- "AI 2027 says humanity ends in 2027." It is a scenario with two endings, explicitly uncertain; the dates are medians with wide ranges.
- "He was fired / blew the whistle on a specific incident." He resigned over loss of confidence and later pushed for a right to warn; he still has confidentiality obligations he signed on joining.
- "The timelines were pushed back." The November 2026 update moved them earlier, not later.
